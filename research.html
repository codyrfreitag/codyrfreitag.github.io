Research

My research background is primarily in cryptography, with an emphasis on cryptographic proofs and their applications to distributed computing. Much of my work explores how to design new cryptographic protocols while understanding the assumptions and limitations underlying their security.

More broadly, I'm interested in applying the tools and perspectives of theoretical cryptography to understand computational systems, particularly those motivated by real-world problems. I'm especially interested in developing well-motivated, precise definitions that capture the properties we care about, and using these definitions to understand what can and cannot be achieved.

Research Areas

Cryptographic Proofs and Verifiable Computation

Cryptographic proofs allow us to verify claims about computations that may be too expensive to perform ourselves or concern private information. Designing these systems involves a tension between efficiency and security, with the guarantees depending on the computational, modeling, and trust assumptions we are willing to make. My research explores these tradeoffs through the construction and analysis of succinct and zero-knowledge proof systems.

Decentralization and Distributed Computation

Technologies such as blockchains have introduced new ways for mutually distrustful parties to perform computational tasks together over the internet, without relying on a trusted central authority. Tools from cryptography provide a principled way for these parties to coordinate securely. My research focuses on fundamental tasks in this setting, such as generating shared randomness and conducting distributed auctions. In particular, I've explored how time-based cryptography, which relies on fine-grained computational assumptions about the time required to solve problems, can be used to circumvent classical impossibility results in distributed computing.

Foundations of Cryptography

At a foundational level, cryptography is about leveraging computational hardness to enable applications that would otherwise be impossible. This motivates studying which cryptographic capabilities are theoretically achievable and what fundamental barriers might prevent them. My research explores these questions through studying advanced cryptographic primitives, such as obfuscation and witness encryption, as well as connections between cryptographic constructions and computational complexity theory.

Modeling and Analyzing Real-World Systems

Real-world computational systems are often large and complex, developed through a series of ad hoc design decisions that prioritize efficiency and usability over rigorous security guarantees. Understanding the security and privacy of these systems requires finding the right abstractions, separating the essential features from incidental complexity so that meaningful guarantees can be established. My work in this direction includes the analysis of hash function designs, differential privacy, and more recently, information leakage in algorithmic trading.
